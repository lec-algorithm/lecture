## Filling in the blanks of a table
::sub[Bonus material that is not in the syllabus. It is not on the exam]

A **recommender system** suggests suitable items based on a user's interests and preferences.

- **Netflix**: movies a user is likely to enjoy
- **Spotify**: personalized music
- **Amazon**: products worth buying together

<br/>

A user × item **rating matrix** is mostly blank.  
Recommending means **predicting the blanks and showing the highest first**.

</Slide>

<Slide class="center">

## 1. Collaborative filtering
::sub[Collaborative Filtering]

</Slide>

<Slide>

#### Finding it in past behavior
::sub[Find similar users or items from ratings and purchase history]

1. **User-based**
   - Recommend items liked by users whose taste is similar to mine.
2. **Item-based**
   - Recommend items similar to the ones I liked.
   - "Similar" is measured not by content but by **the same people's ratings**.

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Strength: no domain knowledge needed
::sub[It need not know what a movie is. It only looks at who liked what]

</Slide>

<Slide>

#### Cosine similarity
::sub[Cosine Similarity · how much two rating vectors point the same way]

$$
\text{cos}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert}
= \frac{\sum_i a_i b_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2} \, \sqrt{\sum_i b_i^2}}
$$

<br/>

- Same direction gives 1, perpendicular 0, opposite -1.
- Dividing by length means it looks not at **how much** but at **in what proportion**.

</Slide>

#### Three steps
::sub[similarity → neighbors → weighted average]

1. Measure the **similarity** between A and every other user.
2. Among users above 0, pick the $$k$$ largest as **neighbors**.
3. For each blank, take the **similarity-weighted average** of the neighbors' ratings.

$$
\hat{r}_{A,i} = \frac{\sum_{v \in N} \text{sim}(A, v) \, r_{v,i}}{\sum_{v \in N} \text{sim}(A, v)}
$$

</Slide>

#### The cosine trap
::sub[B 0.835, C 0.820, E 0.705, D 0.466 · M6 predicted 2.542]

- **D's taste is the exact opposite of A's.**
  - D gave 1 and 2 to movies A rated 5, and 4 to the one A rated 2.
  - Yet the similarity is 0.466.
- **E also moves differently from A.**
  - E gave 5 to M5, which A rated lowest.
  - Yet E became a neighbor and pulled A's M6 prediction up.

<br/>

Ratings are **all positive**, so the cosine can never go below 0.

</Slide>

<Slide>

#### Pearson correlation coefficient
::sub[Pearson Correlation · over movies both rated, after subtracting each one's mean]

$$
\text{pearson}(a, b) = \frac{\sum_{i} (a_i - \bar{a})(b_i - \bar{b})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i} (a_i - \bar{a})^2} \, \sqrt{\sum_{i} (b_i - \bar{b})^2}}
$$

<br/>

All that is left is "above or below their usual?"  
It is the cosine of the mean-subtracted vectors.

</Slide>

#### Translating a harsh rater's scores
::sub[C's 4 is a 5 on A's scale]

Average how far each neighbor **deviates from their own mean**, and add it to A's mean.

$$
\hat{r}_{A,i} = \bar{r}_A + \frac{\sum_{v \in N} \text{sim}(A, v) \, (r_{v,i} - \bar{r}_v)}{\sum_{v \in N} \lvert \text{sim}(A, v) \rvert}
$$

<br/>

The practice code uses the plain weighted average. This formula is left as an exercise.

</Slide>

#### Item-based results
::sub[Average A's own ratings of similar movies, weighted by similarity]

```console
item-based: movies A rated, by cosine to the blank (k = 2)
  M3:  M4 0.845  M1 0.836  M2 0.785  M5 0.761
      -> (0.845*4 + 0.836*5) / (0.845 + 0.836) = 4.497
  M6:  M5 0.909  M4 0.620  M2 0.596  M1 0.562
      -> (0.909*2 + 0.620*4) / (0.909 + 0.620) = 2.811
```

- Without ever looking at genre, action grouped with action and drama with drama.
- Relationships between items barely change, so they are good to **precompute**.
  - Amazon's "customers also bought" is known for working this way.

## Where collaborative filtering falls short
::sub[Sparsity and cold start]

</Slide>

<Slide>

#### Weakness 1. Data sparsity
::sub[Sparsity]

- **It needs a lot of data.**
  - But a lot of data needs a lot of users.
- **Real rating matrices are almost all blank.**
  - The competition dataset Netflix released was close to 99% empty.
  - Pairs that share only one or two movies have similarities hard to trust.

</Slide>

<Slide>

#### Weakness 2. Cold start
::sub[Cold Start · with no history, nothing can be done]

| User A | User B | User C | User E (new) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Roundup: No Way Out | Elemental | 12.12: The Day | Exit |
| Top Gun: Maverick | Aladdin | Hansan: Rising Dragon | |
| Avatar: The Way of Water | Avatar: The Way of Water | Avatar: The Way of Water | |
| Spider-Man: No Way Home | | Spider-Man: No Way Home | |

- **New user**: nobody overlaps with E, so no neighbors and no recommendations.
- **New item**: nobody has seen the new Avengers, so it cannot be recommended to anyone.

</Slide>

#### Content-based results
::sub[`02_content_based_filtering/` · profile O(ng), ranking O(n log n)]

```console
profile of A = sum of (rating - 3) * genres = (5 1 1 1)

score = cosine(profile, genres), movies A has not rated
  1. M3  0.802
  2. M7  0.764
  3. M6  0.189
```

- With no one else's ratings, it reaches **the same conclusion** as Example 1.
- **The new movie M7** came second on genre alone.
  - Something collaborative filtering could not do.

</Slide>

<Slide>

#### Strengths and limits of content-based filtering
::sub[It "somewhat" solves the cold start]

- **It needs analysis of the movies up front.**
  - Not just genre: cast and director work too.
  - Other movies by an actor you like, movies by similar actors
- **New items** can be recommended as long as they have features.
- **New users** still cannot.
  - With no ratings, the profile is the zero vector.
- **It recommends only similar things.**
  - Collaborative filtering is better at crossing genres.

</Slide>

#### How the two differ

| | Collaborative filtering | Content-based filtering |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Looks at | other users' ratings | item features and my ratings |
| Content analysis | not needed | needed |
| Another domain | applies to music or food directly | music needs new analysis |
| New item | cannot recommend | recommends if it has features |
| New user | cannot recommend | cannot recommend |
| Breadth | can cross genres | tends to stick to the similar |

## 3. Hybrid filtering
::sub[Hybrid Filtering · combining the two]

</Slide>

<Slide>

### Try it: the weight α
::sub[α is the share of the collaborative score · in the toy example the ranking flips once α passes 0.75]

<HybridDemo slide />

</Slide>

<Slide class="compact">

#### Mixing the two results with a weight
::sub[Collaborative scores are predicted ratings divided by 5, on a 0–1 scale]

$$
\text{score}_i = \alpha \cdot \text{cf}_i + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{content}_i
$$

```console
hybrid = 0.5 * collaborative + 0.5 * content
  toy: 0.700 0.800 0.350
  A: M3  cf 0.893  content 0.802  -> 0.847
  A: M7  cf  none  content 0.764  -> 0.764
  A: M6  cf 0.307  content 0.189  -> 0.248
```

- The two cover each other's weaknesses. M7 made the list on its content score.
- But both systems have to run, so **the implementation gets more complex**.

</Slide>

## What to keep

1. **Recommending is predicting the blanks of a rating matrix.**
   - Show the highest predictions first.
2. **Collaborative filtering looks only at other people's ratings.**
   - User-based uses similar people, item-based similar items.
3. **With all-positive ratings, cosine is fooled.**
   - Pearson correlation, which subtracts each person's mean, works better.
4. **Content-based filtering compares item features with a profile.**
   - It handles new items but not new users.
5. **Hybrids mix the two scores to fill the gaps.**

</Slide>
