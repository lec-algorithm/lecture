# Case study · The Binance hack

*October 29, 2026: $6.3 billion in 20 minutes*

</Slide>

<Slide>

## The blockchain was not broken
::sub[What failed was people, developer machines, and the links between permissions]

- **Neither cryptography nor the blockchain protocol was broken.**
  - There is no evidence that a private key leaked.
- **It started on one developer's computer.**
  - One installer and one interview task were the way in.
- **Linked permissions led all the way to the signing system.**
  - An internal AI tool, a deployment token, then the signing service.

</Slide>

## Stage 1: looking for an AI tool
::sub[June 2026]

- **A wallet development and operations engineer was looking for an AI coding tool.**
  - The installer found through a search had been tampered with.
- **The security team detected anomalies and replaced the device.**
  - Detection itself worked.
- **But the information had already left.**
  - The work and roles of the engineer and colleagues were exposed to the attacker.
  - In effect, it became the next target list.

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Stage 2: a fake job interview
::sub[September 2026]

- **The target was an engineer who listed Binance on a job platform.**
  - Someone posing as a project director at a well-known company reached out to them.
- **There were two interviews, both with impersonators.**
  - They posed as HR and the CEO, with photos on their accounts.
- **The engineer ran the coding-test repository on a work computer.**
  - Endpoint security did not detect the malware inside.

</Slide>

<Slide>

## What "zero-day malware" means here
::sub[Reading the reported wording carefully]

- **A zero-day vulnerability is one the vendor does not know about yet, or has no patch for.**
  - Zero days are left before a fix.
- **In this case, "zero-day" means malware nobody had seen before.**
  - It was not on any security product's detection list, so it passed straight through.
  - No vulnerability ID (CVE) has been published.
- **A person opened the door.**
  - The victim ran the task code himself.
  - That is why detection tools alone struggle to stop it.

</Slide>

## Stage 3: sideways through an internal AI tool
::sub[October 20 to 29]

- **From the compromised computer, the attacker used an internal AI tool.**
  - Through it, they took over two other engineers' computers.
- **One of them held a container deployment token.**
  - On October 29, the attacker deployed a container with these credentials.
- **That reached the internal wallet signing service.**
  - Signatures could now skip the normal withdrawal process.

</Slide>

<Slide>

## EIP-7702: delegating code to a wallet
::sub[A feature added in Ethereum's 2025 upgrade]

- **An ordinary wallet (EOA) has no code.**
  - It only sends transactions its key owner signs.
- **EIP-7702 lets a wallet delegate to contract code that runs on its behalf.**
  - It was built for conveniences such as batched payments and sponsored fees.
- **The attacker delegated a hot wallet to a sweeper contract.**
  - Even funds arriving later were swept to the attacker.
- **Undoing a delegation takes another signature from the wallet key.**
  - The wallet used multiparty computation (MPC), so no single key could revoke it; it took about 4 hours.

</Slide>

## 20 minutes, 10 chains
::sub[From 20:05 UTC on October 29]

- **Preparation was done the day before.**
  - Three small Tornado Cash withdrawals paid for fees.
  - The code was tested on two wallets and rehearsed on a third.
- **Starting with XRP, most assets left in about 20 minutes.**
  - About 233 million XRP, about 18,270 BTC, about 970,000 SOL
  - About 1.875 billion USDT and 823 million USDC on Ethereum, among others
- **The Ethereum hot wallet was emptied in under 4 minutes.**

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Laundering versus freezing
::sub[Swapping before the issuers can freeze]

- **Stablecoin issuers can freeze tokens.**
  - Tether froze about 1.77 billion USDT and Circle about 825 million USDC.
- **The attacker tried to swap into ETH before the freezes.**
  - About 150 tokens were sold for ETH on decentralized exchanges.
  - USDT was deposited in the lending protocol Aave to borrow ETH.
- **Funds were scattered across more than 800 addresses.**
  - A path mixing them with funds from an earlier Bitget exploit was also found.

</Slide>

<Slide>

## Response
::sub[October 30 to November 3]

- **About $2.85 billion was frozen or blocked.**
  - That is about 44% of the loss.
- **Binance absorbed the losses.**
  - Its user protection fund (SAFU) and company funds replenished about 98%.
- **Withdrawals reopened in phases from November 1.**
  - In the 9 hours after reopening, net outflows were about $3.6 billion.
- **The FBI named North Korea on October 31; North Korea denied it.**

</Slide>

## What we must not do
::sub[The same for students and employees]

- **Do not do personal things on work devices.**
  - Job hunting, interview tasks, and personal downloads stay off work devices.
- **Do not stay signed in to work or school accounts on personal devices.**
- **Do not run code of unknown origin directly on your machine.**
  - Interview tasks, unfamiliar repositories, and files sent over chat all count.
- **Do not download installers from search result links.**
  - Check the official site and the official package name yourself.
- **Do not leave tokens and keys in files, chats, or shell history.**

</Slide>

<Slide>

## What we must do
::sub[So that if one layer fails, the next one stops it]

- **Separate work and personal.**
  - If separate devices are hard, at least separate accounts and browser profiles.
- **Run unknown code in a disposable environment.**
  - This course's lab environment (Codespaces, containers) is exactly that.
- **Sign in with phishing-resistant methods such as hardware security keys.**
- **Give AI tools and tokens only the permissions they need.**
  - Narrow the scope and keep expiry short.
- **Report anything odd right away.**
  - Like the June detection, reporting early is what lets the next stage be cut.

</Slide>

<Slide>

## When a recruiter contacts you
::sub[The situation that opened this case]

- **Verify in reverse through the company's official careers page and email domain.**
  - Do not trust a profile photo and work history alone.
- **Be suspicious of tasks that say "run this code".**
  - If it must run, run it only in a disposable environment.
- **Do not share internal details about your current employer.**
  - Team structure, systems you own, and tools you use are all information.
- **Do not interview with work devices or accounts.**

</Slide>
