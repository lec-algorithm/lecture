> Bonus material that is not in the syllabus. It is not on the exam.  
> When Netflix picks your next movie or Spotify your next song, the job is
> really **filling in the blanks of a table**.  
> There are two clues for filling a blank: people similar to you, and the
> properties of the item itself.  
> Both come down to one similarity between vectors, plus sorting.

## What a recommender system is \{#overview}

A **recommender system** suggests suitable items based on a user's
interests and preferences. They are everywhere.

- **Netflix**: movies a user is likely to enjoy
- **Spotify**: personalized music
- **Amazon**: products worth buying together

Put users in rows and items in columns, and write ratings in the cells: that
is a **rating matrix**. Any one person sees only a tiny fraction of all
movies, so the matrix is mostly blank. Recommending means **predicting the
ratings that belong in the blanks and showing the highest predictions
first**. There are two broad ways to fill a blank.

- **Collaborative filtering**: use other people's ratings as the clue.
- **Content-based filtering**: use the item's features (genre, cast,
  director) as the clue.

## Collaborative filtering \{#collaborative}

Collaborative filtering recommends by finding similar users or items from
**users' past behavior** (ratings, purchase history). It comes in two
flavors.

- **User-based**: recommend items liked by users whose taste is similar to
  mine.
- **Item-based**: recommend items similar to the ones I liked. Here
  "similar" is measured not by content but by **whether the same people
  rated them alike**.

## Similarity as a number: cosine similarity \{#cosine}

"Number of shared movies" is crude. It cannot tell a 5-star rating from a
1-star one. Treat one user's ratings as a vector and measure **how much two
vectors point the same way**, and the size of the ratings counts too. That
is **cosine similarity**.

$$
\text{cos}(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert}
= \frac{\sum_i a_i b_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2} \, \sqrt{\sum_i b_i^2}}
$$

- Same direction gives 1, perpendicular gives 0, opposite gives -1.
- Dividing by the lengths means it looks not at **how much** someone rated
  but at **in what proportion**.

Measure it on a small matrix: 4 users, 4 items, and 0
means not rated yet.

| | item 1 | item 2 | item 3 | item 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| user A | 5 | 3 | 0 | 0 |
| user B | 4 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| user C | 0 | 0 | 5 | 4 |
| user D | 0 | 0 | 4 | 0 |

A and B both have 0 for items 3 and 4, so we can draw them as they are in
the item 1–item 2 plane. Drag the tips around. Change only the length,
keeping the direction, and the cosine stays the same; put one vector on an
axis to make a right angle and it drops to 0. Ratings are never negative, so
the two vectors can never be more than 90 degrees apart.

<CosineDemo />

$$
\text{cos}(A, B) = \frac{5 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 0}{\sqrt{5^2 + 3^2} \, \sqrt{4^2}}
= \frac{20}{5.831 \times 4} = 0.857
$$

The same way, C and D give $$\dfrac{20}{\sqrt{41} \times 4} = 0.781$$, while
A and C share no item at all, so their dot product and similarity are 0. The
first table of the practice code is this computation.

## Example 1. User-based collaborative filtering \{#user-based}

Now a slightly bigger matrix, all the way to a prediction. 5 users, 6
movies, and **A's M3 and M6 are blank**. Filling those two cells decides
what to recommend to A.

| | M1 | M2 | M3 | M4 | M5 | M6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 5 | 5 | **?** | 4 | 2 | **?** |
| B | 5 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 1 |
| C | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 1 | 2 |
| D | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 4 | 5 |
| E | 4 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 5 |

M1 The Roundup: No Way Out · M2 Top Gun: Maverick · M3 Avatar: The Way of
Water · M4 Spider-Man: No Way Home · M5 Elemental · M6 12.12: The Day

A gave high scores to the action movies (M1, M2, M4) and a low score to
*Elemental* (M5). The procedure has three steps.

1. Measure the **similarity** between A and every other user.
2. Among users with similarity above 0, pick the $$k$$ largest as
   **neighbors**.
3. For each blank, take the **similarity-weighted average** of the
   neighbors' ratings. More similar neighbors count more.

$$
\hat{r}_{A,i} = \frac{\sum_{v \in N} \text{sim}(A, v) \, r_{v,i}}{\sum_{v \in N} \text{sim}(A, v)}
$$

### Picking neighbors by cosine \{#user-cosine}

First run it with the same cosine similarity as above and $$k = 3$$.
Similarities pile up in the right column, and users chosen as neighbors
turn green. At the end, only the neighbors' ratings in the blank's column
remain and are averaged.

<CollabFilterDemo measure="cosine" />

B 0.835, C 0.820, E 0.705, D 0.466. The neighbors are B, C and E, and M3
comes out 4.354 and M6 **2.542**. But these numbers are a little odd.

- **D's taste is the exact opposite of A's.** D gave 1 and 2 to the movies
  A rated 5, and 4 to the movie A rated 2. Yet the similarity is a fairly
  high 0.466.
- **E also moves differently from A.** E gave its highest score, 5, to M5,
  which A rated lowest. Yet at 0.705 E became a neighbor, and the 5 E gave
  M6 pulled A's M6 prediction up.

Ratings run from 1 to 5, so they are **all positive**. Positive vectors can
never be more than 90 degrees apart, so the cosine can never go below 0,
and even opposite tastes look fairly similar.

See it in a picture. On the left is a plane made of two movies A rated, and the
bars on the right are the cosine over all six cells, with unrated cells left at 0.
Use the slider to subtract c from every rated cell.

<OriginShiftDemo />

At $$c = 0$$ every arrow is stuck in the upper-right quadrant and every bar is
above 0. In the M1·M5 plane at $$c = 3$$, A is $$(2, -1)$$ and D is $$(-2, 1)$$,
**exactly opposite (180 degrees)**, and D's six-cell cosine falls to -0.671.
People use different scales, so subtracting one value from everyone is still crude.

### Pearson correlation \{#pearson}

The fix is to **subtract each person's own mean** before measuring. Then all
that is left is "did they rate this above or below their usual?" The cosine
measured this way, over movies both have rated, is the **Pearson correlation
coefficient**.

$$
\text{pearson}(a, b) = \frac{\sum_{i} (a_i - \bar{a})(b_i - \bar{b})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i} (a_i - \bar{a})^2} \, \sqrt{\sum_{i} (b_i - \bar{b})^2}}
$$

The sums run only over movies **both have rated**, and $$\bar{a}$$ and
$$\bar{b}$$ are the means over those movies too.

Look at it as bars first. Pick a user to compare and click **Minus the mean**.
Only the movies both have rated remain, and each bar drops by its user's mean,
so a movie rated below one's usual hangs below 0. With C the two sets of bars
take exactly the same shape; with D and E they point opposite ways.

<PearsonDemo />

Now work it out by hand. The movies A and each of the other four have both
rated are M1, M2, M4 and M5.

| | M1 | M2 | M4 | M5 | mean | minus mean | Pearson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 5 | 5 | 4 | 2 | 4 | (1, 1, 0, -2) | |
| B | 5 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 4 | (1, 0, 0, -1) | 0.866 |
| C | 4 | 4 | 3 | 1 | 3 | (1, 1, 0, -2) | 1.000 |
| D | 1 | 2 | 1 | 4 | 2 | (-1, 0, -1, 2) | -0.833 |
| E | 4 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 4 | (0, -1, 0, 1) | -0.866 |

- **C has exactly A's taste; C just rates one point lower.** Subtracting the
  mean gives the same vector as A's, so 1.000. By cosine C was 0.820, below B.
- A and E give $$\dfrac{0 - 1 + 0 - 2}{\sqrt{6} \times \sqrt{2}} = -0.866$$:
  **the opposite taste shows up as a negative number.** D is -0.833.

Run the same demo with Pearson. Only cells both have rated stay lit.

<CollabFilterDemo />

The only neighbors above 0 are C and B. Even with $$k = 3$$, the negative D
and E are left out.

$$
\hat{r}_{A,\text{M3}} = \frac{1.000 \times 4 + 0.866 \times 5}{1.000 + 0.866} = 4.464,
\qquad
\hat{r}_{A,\text{M6}} = \frac{1.000 \times 2 + 0.866 \times 1}{1.000 + 0.866} = 1.536
$$

- Pseudocode

```plaintext
ALGORITHM PearsonSimilarity(a, b, n)
    common <- the i with a[i] > 0 and b[i] > 0   // only items both rated
    if common is empty then return 0
    ma <- mean of a over common,  mb <- mean of b over common
    num <- 0,  da <- 0,  db <- 0
    for i in common do                       // subtract each one's mean
        num <- num + (a[i] - ma) * (b[i] - mb)
        da <- da + (a[i] - ma)^2
        db <- db + (b[i] - mb)^2
    if da = 0 or db = 0 then return 0
    return num / sqrt(da * db)

ALGORITHM PredictUserBased(R, sim, neighbors, item)
    num <- 0,  den <- 0
    for v in neighbors do                    // top k with sim > 0
        if R[v][item] > 0 then               // if the neighbor rated it
            num <- num + sim[v] * R[v][item] // the more similar, the more weight
            den <- den + sim[v]
    if den = 0 then return 0
    return num / den                         // similarity-weighted average
```

- C implementation (excerpt)

```c
double predictUserBased(const int ratings[][ITEMS], const double sim[],
                        const int neighbors[], int count, int item) {
    double num = 0.0, den = 0.0;
    for (int i = 0; i < count; i++) {
        int v = neighbors[i];
        if (ratings[v][item] > 0) {
            num += sim[v] * ratings[v][item];
            den += sim[v];
        }
    }
    return den == 0.0 ? 0.0 : num / den;
}
```

`sqrt` from `math.h` needs an extra link option (`-lm`) on Linux, so the
practice code carries its own `squareRoot` that finds square roots by
Newton's method. Python uses the same function, so the outputs match to the
last character.

**Cost**: with $$m$$ users and $$n$$ items, one similarity is $$O(n)$$, and
the target user against everyone else is $$O(mn)$$. Precomputing every pair
of users costs $$O(m^2 n)$$. With hundreds of millions of users, that square
becomes the problem.

### Translating a harsh rater's scores \{#mean-centered}

Pearson picked C as a neighbor, but the weighted average still uses C's
**raw ratings**. C rates one point lower than A, so C's 4 is a 5 on A's
scale. That is why the formula used in practice averages how far each
neighbor **deviates from their own mean**, and adds that to the target
user's mean.

$$
\hat{r}_{A,i} = \bar{r}_A + \frac{\sum_{v \in N} \text{sim}(A, v) \, (r_{v,i} - \bar{r}_v)}{\sum_{v \in N} \lvert \text{sim}(A, v) \rvert}
$$

The practice code uses the plain weighted average and leaves this formula
as an exercise.

Run the two formulas side by side. The neighbors are C and B, picked by Pearson,
and each mean is taken over all the movies that person rated (A 4, B 3.667, C 3).
**Step** walks through the mean-centered formula, and the slider raises all of C's
ratings.

<MeanCenterDemo />

The mean-centered formula gives M3 **5.155** and M6 **2.226**; the simple weighted
mean gives 4.464 and 1.536. Raise C by +1 and Pearson stays 1.000, while the simple
weighted mean moves to M3 5.000 and M6 2.072 and the mean-centered value does not
move. **Getting the same answer on a different scale** is the point of this formula.
A value off the scale, like 5.155, is cut to 5 when used.

## Where collaborative filtering falls short \{#cf-limits}

### Data sparsity \{#sparsity}

Collaborative filtering needs a lot of data, and a lot of data needs a lot
of users. This is the **data sparsity** problem.

- Real rating matrices are almost all blank. The competition dataset
  Netflix once released was close to 99% empty.
- So pairs that share only one or two rated movies are common, and their
  similarity is hard to trust.

Erase cells yourself. This is synthetic data with three taste groups (60 movies),
and 10% of the cells are held back as test cells whose answers are known. Every
slider move really recomputes all pairs' Pearson and the test-cell predictions
(Pearson, $$k = 5$$).

<SparsityDemo />

With 40 users at 59.8% blank, every test cell can be predicted, with an error of
0.588. At 81.1% blank, pairs share 2.1 movies on average, predictable cells fall to
68.8%, and the error of 1.206 is worse even than guessing the overall mean (1.178).
At 95.7% blank, no cell can be predicted.

- **Pearson over two shared movies cannot be trusted.** Two points always fit a
  line, so the result is +1 or -1, or else the mean-subtracted values are all 0 and
  it cannot be measured. At 81.1% blank, counting those 216 pairs gives 96 at ±1 and
  120 unmeasurable, and no other value.
- **Many users can hold up.** At the same 80% or so, 320 users make 99.9% of cells
  predictable with an error of 0.823. That is why collaborative filtering needs many
  users.

### Cold start \{#cold-start}

With no history, nothing can be done. This is the **cold start** problem,
and it happens on both sides, people and items.

- **New user**: user E just signed up and has watched only *Exit*. E shares
  no movie with anyone, so every similarity is 0, and there are no
  neighbors and no recommendations.

| User A | User B | User C | User E (new) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Roundup: No Way Out | Elemental | 12.12: The Day | Exit |
| Top Gun: Maverick | Aladdin | Hansan: Rising Dragon | |
| Avatar: The Way of Water | Avatar: The Way of Water | Avatar: The Way of Water | |
| Spider-Man: No Way Home | | Spider-Man: No Way Home | |

- **New item**: a new Avengers movie has just opened. Nobody has seen it,
  so its column is all blank, and neither user-based nor item-based
  filtering can recommend it to anyone.

## Example 2. Content-based filtering \{#content-based}

Content-based filtering recommends, based on **the item's own attributes**
(features), other items whose features resemble the ones the user liked. If
the movies I liked were action movies, it recommends other action movies.
Netflix rows like "Action movies for you" and "Action comedies for you" are
this shape.

### Genre vectors and taste vectors \{#genre-vector}

Give each movie a vector that marks its genres with 0 and 1. A small example
uses three genres (action, comedy, drama). The user likes action
and comedy, so the taste vector is (1, 1, 0).

| movie | action | comedy | drama | cosine with taste (1, 1, 0) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| movie 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0.707 |
| movie 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1.000 |
| movie 3 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0.000 |

Movie 2, action plus comedy, comes first, and movie 1, action only, second
at $$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707$$. It is cosine similarity again.

### Building a profile from ratings \{#profile}

Users do not write down their taste vectors. We build one from the ratings
they gave. For each movie watched, add its genres **scaled by (rating - 3)**.
A 3 is neutral and adds 0; genres of movies rated high go +, genres of
movies rated low go -. This is the **user profile**.

Try it on A from Example 1. Add one more genre (sci-fi), and add **M7, which
nobody has rated yet** because it just opened (the new Avengers).

| movie | action | comedy | drama | sci-fi | A's rating | weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M1 The Roundup: No Way Out | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 5 | +2 |
| M2 Top Gun: Maverick | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 5 | +2 |
| M3 Avatar: The Way of Water | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | | |
| M4 Spider-Man: No Way Home | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 4 | +1 |
| M5 Elemental | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 2 | -1 |
| M6 12.12: The Day | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | | |
| M7 the new Avengers | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | | |

The profile builds up in the bottom row, then each unwatched movie gets its
cosine with the profile on the right.

<ContentFilterDemo />

The profile is $$(5, 1, 1, 1)$$. Action dominates. The low score for
*Elemental* knocked 1 off comedy and drama.

- Pseudocode

```plaintext
ALGORITHM BuildProfile(F, r, n, g)
    profile <- g zeros
    for i <- 0 to n-1 do
        if r[i] > 0 then
            w <- r[i] - 3                   // 3 is neutral, higher +, lower -
            for t <- 0 to g-1 do
                profile[t] <- profile[t] + w * F[i][t]
    return profile

ALGORITHM RankByContent(F, r, n, g, profile)
    candidates <- []
    for i <- 0 to n-1 do
        if r[i] = 0 then                    // unrated items only (new ones too)
            score[i] <- CosineSimilarity(profile, F[i], g)
            append i to candidates
    sort candidates by score, descending
    return candidates
```

- C implementation (excerpt)

```c
void buildProfile(const double features[][GENRES], const int ratings[],
                  int n, int g, double profile[]) {
    for (int t = 0; t < g; t++) {
        profile[t] = 0.0;
    }
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
        if (ratings[i] > 0) {
            int w = ratings[i] - 3;
            for (int t = 0; t < g; t++) {
                profile[t] += w * features[i][t];
            }
        }
    }
}
```

### Strengths and limits of content-based filtering \{#content-limits}

- **It needs analysis up front.** Someone has to decide first whether a
  movie is action or comedy. We used only genres here, but cast and
  director work too.
  - Other movies starring an actor you like
  - Movies starring actors similar to one you like
- **It solves the cold start for new items.** With features, an item can be
  scored without any ratings.
- **The cold start for new users remains.** With no ratings, the profile is
  the zero vector and every score is 0. That is why it only "somewhat"
  solves cold start.
- **It recommends only similar things.** Someone who liked action is shown
  only action, so they have fewer chances to meet good movies outside their
  taste. Collaborative filtering can make genre-crossing recommendations,
  like the *12.12: The Day* that C watched.

## How the two differ \{#comparison}

| | Collaborative filtering | Content-based filtering |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Looks at | other users' ratings | item features and my ratings |
| Content analysis | not needed | needed |
| Moving to another domain | methods studied on movies apply directly to music or food | music needs new analysis such as genre and creator |
| New item | cannot recommend | recommends if it has features |
| New user | cannot recommend | cannot recommend |
| Breadth | can cross genres | tends to get stuck on the similar |

With collaborative filtering it is also **easy to change the method
itself**. This note used cosine and Pearson, but you can experiment with a
different similarity, a different neighbor rule, or deliberately mixing in
lower predictions. The procedure does not care what the items are.

## Hybrid filtering \{#hybrid}

**Hybrid filtering** combines the two approaches. The simplest way is to
mix the two results with a weight.

$$
\text{score}_i = \alpha \cdot \text{cf}_i + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{content}_i
$$

A small example first mixes collaborative scores (0.8, 0.7, 0.3) and
content scores (0.6, 0.9, 0.4) for three items. For A,
we divide the Pearson predictions from Example 1 by 5 to put them on a 0–1
scale and mix them with the scores from Example 2. M7 has no collaborative
score, so it uses its content score as is.

Move $$\alpha$$. Each bar stacks the collaborative share and the content
share, and the rows swap places by score. In the toy example item 1 overtakes
item 2 once $$\alpha$$ goes above 0.75. A's order stays M3, M7, M6 whatever
$$\alpha$$ is.

- M3, rated high by both, is firmly first.
- The new movie M7 made the list thanks to its content score. Once ratings

- The two cover each other's weaknesses, but you have to run both systems
  and choose $$\alpha$$, so **the implementation gets more complex**.

Real services also switch rather than add: use content-based filtering for
a new user, then move to collaborative filtering once enough history builds
up.

## What to keep from this topic \{#takeaway}

- Recommending is **predicting the blanks of a rating matrix and showing the
  highest first**.
- **Collaborative filtering** looks only at other people's ratings.
  User-based averages similar people's ratings; item-based averages my own
  ratings of similar items; both weight by similarity.
- **Cosine similarity** measures the direction of two vectors. When ratings
  are all positive, even opposite tastes look similar, so **Pearson
  correlation, which subtracts each person's mean**, works better.
- The weaknesses of collaborative filtering are **sparsity and cold start**.
  It can recommend neither to new users nor new items.
- **Content-based filtering** compares item features with a user profile.
  It handles new items but not new users, and needs content analysis first.
- **Hybrids** mix the two scores to fill each other's gaps.
